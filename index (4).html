What California's new deletion tool removes, and what it leaves behind

Since August 1, every data broker registered in California has had to check a state list and delete the people on it. Then it has to keep deleting them every 45 days. More than half a million Californians have signed up. DROP is the most powerful deletion tool any state has built. It is also narrower than it sounds. Here is what one request reaches, what it cannot touch, and what is still left for you to do.

D David
October 2026
~13 min read
Share
For years, getting your name off data broker sites meant doing it one site at a time. Find the listing, fill out the form, confirm the email, and check back months later to see whether you came back. When Consumer Reports tested that process in 2024, opting out by hand removed about 70 percent of profiles within a week. The best paid removal services managed about two-thirds after four months. And every participant was still listed somewhere at every checkpoint. [1]

California's answer is DROP, the Delete Request and Opt-out Platform. One request goes to every registered data broker in the state, and the law makes them keep honoring it. I went through the statute, the technical guidance the state gave brokers, and the state's own numbers to see what that request actually does.

DROP is worth the ten minutes it takes. It is also the tool least likely to touch the part of your profile people actually see. That is the listing that comes up when someone searches your name.

How One Request Reaches 600 Brokers

DROP comes from the Delete Act, a 2023 California law written by State Senator Josh Becker. [2] It required the California Privacy Protection Agency, which now goes by CalPrivacy, to build a single deletion system for every registered broker. Californians could start filing on January 1, 2026, and 18,000 did in the first 48 hours. [3]

Who Counts as a Data Broker
California defines a data broker as a business that knowingly collects and sells the personal information of people it has no direct relationship with. Brokers have to register with the state every year, and the 2026 fee is $6,000. [4]

The registry listed 459 brokers in June 2025. A year later it listed 581, the most since it began in 2020. By late August, 654 brokers were part of DROP. [2] [3] [5] Not all of them are obscure. Bloomberg Law's review of the 2026 registry found General Motors, T-Mobile, and LexisNexis on it. [6]
What You Give It
You confirm that you live in California through the state's identity service, then enter your details. The minimum is your name, date of birth, and ZIP code. You can also add other names you have gone by, such as a maiden name, and more identifiers. Those include email addresses, phone numbers, your phone's advertising ID, the ID your smart TV uses for ads, and your car's VIN. [7]

It takes most people under ten minutes. [5] You can file for someone else, such as a parent, as long as you verify their residency too. [8] The request stays active until you withdraw it, and it automatically covers brokers that register later. [7] [9]
What Brokers Actually Receive Brokers never see your details in plain text. They see hashes, and they have to produce the same hashes from their own records to find you. A hash only matches an exact copy. A broker that has you under an old ZIP code or a nickname will not find you through that list.
DROP first standardizes each identifier. Names become lowercase, dates become eight digits, and phone numbers become ten. Then it runs each one through SHA-256 and sorts the results into six lists. A broker standardizes and hashes its own records the same way and looks for identical values. [10]
Name, birth date, ZIPYour first name, last name, date of birth, and ZIP code, hashed together. Every request includes this one.
EmailEach address you add, hashed on its own.
PhoneEach number, as ten digits.
Ad IDYour phone's mobile advertising ID, the number apps use to tie your activity together for advertisers.
Name and VINYour name paired with your car's vehicle identification number.
TV IDThe advertising ID your smart TV sends.
That is why the extra names and ZIP codes matter. DROP builds a separate combination for each version of your name and each ZIP code you enter. Every combination is another chance to match. [10]

It is also worth being clear about what hashing does here. It is a way to compare lists, not a lock. A ten-digit phone number hashed this way can be reversed by brute force in minutes on ordinary hardware. And any broker that already has your name, birth date, and ZIP code can compute the same value. That is the point. It lets brokers find you without the state handing them your details in plain text.
What Brokers Have to Do This is the part opting out by hand never had.
Since August 1, 2026, every registered broker has had to download the lists at least once every 45 days. For each person it matches, it has to delete all the information it holds that the law does not exempt, including the inferences it drew from that data. It also has to tell its service providers and contractors to do the same. [10] [11] After that, it has to delete that person again on every 45-day cycle, and it cannot sell or share new information about them. [11]

Sometimes an identifier matches more than one person, such as a phone number a household shares. Then the broker cannot delete, so it has to opt everyone tied to that identifier out of sale and sharing instead. A broker that fails to process a request faces a fine of $200 per day, per person. [10] Starting in 2028, every broker also has to pass an independent compliance audit every three years. [11]
Why the 45-day cycle matters Removals by hand have always had a shelf life. Brokers keep buying fresh data, and a listing you removed can quietly come back months later. CalPrivacy calls DROP a first-of-its-kind deletion mechanism, and the 45-day cycle is a big part of why. [12] For registered brokers, it keeps deleting you without you having to file again.

What Happens After You File

Brokers had until September 14 to download the first lists, and then 45 days to report back. So the first full round of results should land by the end of October. [10] You can watch it happen on a status page, broker by broker. [2] Each broker reports one of four results, and they do not all mean what most people assume.

The Four Results Until a broker reports, it shows as Pending. Brokers have up to 90 days to post a result. [7]
DeletedThe broker found you and deleted everything the law did not let it keep.
ExemptedThe broker found you, but everything it holds falls under a legal exemption, so it keeps all of it.
Opted outThe broker could not tie the data to you alone. It keeps the data, and it can no longer sell or share it.
Record not foundThe broker has nothing on you, or it could not match what you entered. The status does not tell you which. [7]
The Numbers So Far
More than 520,000 Californians had signed up by mid-September. [13] About a quarter of brokers had reported results by late August, before the deadline for the first download had even passed. Those brokers had deleted "tens of millions of records." The typical user's information had been removed by more than 40 of them. [2]

Most brokers will never have heard of any one person, so 40 out of more than 600 is not a failure rate. It is a reminder that a request sent to 600 brokers and a profile deleted by 600 brokers are not the same thing.
"Deleted" is not the same as gone California's privacy law does not count publicly available information as personal information at all. [14] A people-search site can delete the phone numbers and emails it bought from marketers, report Deleted, and keep everything it pulled from public records. Your status page can say Deleted while your listing is still up.

What DROP Leaves Behind

None of these are mistakes. They are written into the law, and CalPrivacy lists most of them itself. [7] Together, though, they cover a large share of what people mean when they say they want their information off the internet.

Public Records This is most of what a people-search listing is built on.
Under California law, information "lawfully made available from federal, state, or local government records" is not personal information. CalPrivacy lists publicly available data under what DROP will not delete. [7] [14] For a people-search site, that is much of the product. The address on your deed, the value of your home, court cases with your name on them, and professional licenses all come from government records. As KQED put it in its guide to DROP, publicly available information such as court appearances and property records is not deleted. [13]
Companies You Deal With Directly The companies with the most data on you are not brokers when it comes to you.
A business is only a data broker for people it has no direct relationship with. [4] What you gave Google, Meta, Amazon, your bank, or your phone carrier is what CalPrivacy calls first-party data. A DROP request does not require any of them to delete it. [7] The same company can be both. T-Mobile is on the broker registry, but if you are a T-Mobile customer, what it knows from your account is first-party data. [6] For these companies, you still have to use your ordinary California deletion rights, one company at a time.
Credit, Banking, and Health Data Exempt by law, so it stays even when a broker finds you.
Data covered by other privacy laws is exempt from deletion. That includes credit reports under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, financial data under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, and medical information under HIPAA. Companies are not treated as brokers at all to the extent their activity falls under those laws. [11] [15] A broker that holds only exempt data about you will report Exempted and keep all of it. [7] Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion are not going to delete your credit file because you filed a DROP request.
Brokers That Never Registered DROP only reaches the brokers the state knows about.
DROP works off the registry, so a broker that never registered never downloads the lists. CalPrivacy has brought more than a dozen enforcement actions against unregistered brokers. In September it fined a Virginia company, SalesIntel, $36,400 and ordered it to start processing DROP requests. [12] Every broker the state has not caught yet is still outside the system. That same month, the agency warned brokers that it will fine them for inaccurate registry filings, after its enforcement staff found errors. [16]
Copies Already Sold A deletion stops the next sale, not the last one.
The Delete Act tells brokers to pass a deletion on to their service providers and contractors. It does not tell them to recall copies they already sold. [11]

That matters more than it used to. Brokers now have to disclose in their registry filings who they sold or shared data with in the past year. That includes the federal government, law enforcement, foreign actors, and AI developers. [16] The DNS company Control D analyzed the 2026 filings. It found that 55 brokers reported selling or sharing with the federal government, and 28 with law enforcement without a subpoena or court order. The figures are self-reported. [17]

Whatever reached a government agency before you filed stays there. I covered what agencies do with that data in my earlier piece on the government buying data it cannot legally collect.
If you do not live in California DROP is only open to verified California residents. [9] Everywhere else, the old process is all there is. Find each listing and opt out one site at a time. The steps at the end of this piece work anywhere, except the first one.

DROP vs. Doing It Yourself

DROP does not replace opting out by hand, and opting out by hand does not replace DROP. They cover different ground, and paid removal services sit somewhere in between.

Which Brokers It Reaches
DROPALL REGISTERED Every broker on the state registry, more than 600 at once. [2] Nothing outside it.
By handWHAT YOU FIND Only the sites you find and work through yourself, registered or not.
Paid servicesTHEIR LIST Whichever sites the service covers.
People-Search Listings Built From Public Records
DROPNOT REQUIRED Brokers can keep anything that came from government records. [7]
By handBEST RESULTS Many sites will take a listing down through their own opt-out form. In Consumer Reports' test, opting out by hand removed about 70 percent of profiles within a week. [1]
Paid servicesMIXED The same forms, filed for you. After four months, the best services in the test had removed about two-thirds of profiles, and the worst had removed under 10 percent. [1]
Staying Removed
DROPAUTOMATIC Brokers have to delete you again every 45 days and cannot sell new data about you. [11]
By handNONE Nothing stops a site from listing you again. You have to keep checking.
Paid servicesVARIES Depends on the service, and it stops when you stop paying.
Cost and Effort
DROPFree. About ten minutes, once. [5]
By handFree, but hours of searching, forms, and follow-up, repeated over time.
Paid services$20 to $250 a year in Consumer Reports' test. [1]
The point is not to pick one DROP is the only option that keeps working after you file. Opting out by hand is the only one that reaches public-record listings and the brokers the state has not found. Most people need both.

What I Would Do

1
File a DROP request, and give it everything
Go to privacy.ca.gov/drop. Enter the names you have gone by, the ZIP codes you have lived in, and the email addresses and phone numbers you have used. Each one is another chance for a broker's hash to match yours. Add your phone's advertising ID before you reset or delete it, because the old ID is the one brokers have on file. CalPrivacy explains how to find it. [5]
2
Read your results like a skeptic
When the first round lands, remember that Deleted means the broker removed what the law required, not that your listing is gone. Search your name on the big people-search sites and see what is still up. If a site that clearly has you reports Record not found, update your request with the details that listing shows. [8] [18]
3
Remove the public-record listings by hand
Use each site's own opt-out form for whatever is still up. In Consumer Reports' test, this removed more profiles, faster, than any paid service. [1] Keep a list of every site you were on, because you will be checking it again.
4
Freeze your credit
The credit bureaus are outside DROP entirely. A freeze at Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion is free and does not affect your score. It makes it much harder for anyone to open new credit in your name. [19] While you are at it, use optoutprescreen.com to stop the bureaus from using your file for prescreened credit and insurance offers. [20]
5
Stop the next copy from being made
Turn on Global Privacy Control in your browser. California businesses have to treat it as a request not to sell or share your data. Firefox, Brave, and DuckDuckGo support it now. Starting in January 2027, a new California law requires every browser to offer a setting like it. [21] [22] For the companies you use directly, send deletion requests through their own privacy pages, because DROP will not do it for you.
6
If someone has threatened you, look into Safe at Home
California's Safe at Home program gives people who fear for their safety a substitute address to use in public records. That includes survivors of domestic violence and stalking, and some health care workers and patients. [23] It is the only tool here that keeps your home address out of the records people-search sites are built from.
7
Check again in three months
DROP keeps working in the background for registered brokers. Nothing watches the rest. Search yourself every few months, and expect to find something.
The real message DROP is the first deletion tool in the country that keeps working after you file. Every Californian should use it. Just do not mistake a status page full of Deleted for being off the internet. The listing a stranger finds when they search your name is built from public records. Removing that part is still up to you.
