Why privacy matters, even when you have nothing to hide

The "nothing to hide" argument assumes privacy is about covering up wrongdoing. The people in this piece weren't hiding anything. Their own location data made them suspects, and their home addresses were a search away. Privacy is about who gets to learn what about you, and what they can do with it. American law leaves most of that to the market.

Share
In December 2018, police in Avondale, Arizona, arrested Jorge Molina on suspicion of murder at the Macy's warehouse where he worked. Google location data had tracked his phone to the place where a man was shot nine months earlier. A security camera had also caught someone firing from a white Honda Civic, the same model Molina owned.

"I just kept thinking, You're innocent, so you're going to get out," he told the New York Times. After almost a week in jail, the case fell apart. A friend said she'd been with him at home, and texts and Uber receipts backed her up. Police later arrested his mother's ex-boyfriend, who sometimes used Molina's car. By then Molina had lost his job, and his car had been impounded and repossessed. [1]

Molina had nothing to hide, and it didn't protect him. He never thought about what his phone was recording, so he had no idea what it said about him until the police read it their way. That's what the "nothing to hide" argument misses. It treats privacy as cover for wrongdoing, so only people with something to hide would need it. But privacy was never mainly about secrets. The risk comes from ordinary information in the hands of someone who reads it their own way.

This is the piece behind everything else on this site. It covers what the argument gets wrong, what's out there about you, why the law allows it, what it all adds up to and what you can do. The other articles go deeper into particular tools and laws. This one explains why any of them matter.

Part I: What the Argument Gets Wrong

The argument goes like this. If you aren't doing anything wrong, you have nothing to fear from being watched. It sounds reasonable, but it rests on three assumptions, and none of them holds up.

It assumes only the guilty get caught
Zachary McCoy found out from an email that police wanted his data. In January 2020, Google told him that police in Gainesville, Florida, had demanded information tied to his account. It would release the data in seven days unless he went to court. A fitness app had been logging his bike rides. On the day a 97-year-old woman's home was burglarized, his route had passed her house three times within an hour. It was one of his usual loops. His parents paid for a lawyer, prosecutors withdrew the warrant, and police dropped him as a suspect. [2]

Like Molina, McCoy had done nothing wrong. His data pointed at him anyway, because data doesn't explain itself. Someone else decides what it means.
It assumes the only one watching is the government
In July 2020, a gunman found Judge Esther Salas's home in North Brunswick, New Jersey, through personal information posted online. He came to the house and opened fire, killing her 20-year-old son, Daniel Anderl, and wounding her husband. [3]

In June 2025, Minnesota state Representative Melissa Hortman and her husband were killed at home, and another lawmaker and his wife were shot. In the suspect's car, police found a list of 11 data broker sites, with notes on which ones were free. Hortman's address was written next to her name. [4]

The argument usually pictures a government agent reading your files. The people who came for these families didn't need one, because the information was online. Neither family was hiding anything. What put them in danger was an address that was easy to find.
It assumes privacy means secrecy
The legal scholar Daniel Solove has written one of the best-known answers to the argument. Its "deeper problem," he says, is that it "myopically views privacy as a form of secrecy." Most of what's collected about you isn't secret. Your address, your commute and the clinic you visit are ordinary facts. The harm comes from what Solove calls aggregation, "the fusion of small bits of seemingly innocuous data" into something revealing. It also comes from what he calls exclusion, when you can't know how information about you is being used. [5]

So the better question isn't whether you have something to hide. It's who gets to know what about you, and what they can do with it.

Part II: What's Out There

Almost none of this involves anyone breaking the law. Most of it is collected, combined and sold as a normal part of doing business.

Three facts can identify most Americans
In 2000, Latanya Sweeney, then at Carnegie Mellon, showed that a five-digit ZIP code, a birth date and a person's sex were enough to single out 87 percent of Americans. [6]

Three years earlier, as a graduate student at MIT, she had found Massachusetts Governor William Weld's medical records in hospital data the state had released with the names removed. She did it by matching that data against a Cambridge voter list she bought for $20. The demonstration shaped how HIPAA, the federal health privacy law, defines de-identified data. Weld was easier to find than most people, since his hospital stay had been in the news, but the point held. [7] Facts that mean nothing alone can identify you once someone combines them.
Your address is for sale
People-search sites sell profiles of ordinary people. A listing can show your current and past addresses, phone numbers and relatives, and anyone can look you up. [4]

California has required data brokers to register since 2020. In June 2026 its registry listed a record 581 of them. Those are only the ones that registered. [8] There's no federal registry at all. In December 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau proposed a rule that would have brought some brokers under the federal credit reporting law. It withdrew the rule in May 2025. [9]

The sites say the information is public, and piece by piece much of it is. A deed sits in a county office. A phone number leaks in a breach. A birth date sits on a voter roll. Put on one page that anyone can search, it becomes a way to find a person.

The privacy scholar Helen Nissenbaum explains why that matters. Information carries the norms of the setting it came from. Moving it into a different setting can violate those norms, even when every piece was technically public. [10]
Brokers have long sold to the government
In 2004, Chris Hoofnagle, now at Berkeley Law, showed how ChoicePoint and other brokers built files on Americans for law enforcement. He drew on more than 1,500 documents from nine federal agencies. He argued that the Privacy Act, which limits the government's own databases, should cover the brokers too. [11] Congress never made that change. Agencies still buy from brokers what they would need a warrant to collect, and I go through that in my piece on government data purchases.
Your phone keeps a record of your days
Your phone reports where it is to its operating system, to the apps you install and to the advertising code inside those apps. Much of that ends up with brokers. In 2024, the FTC acted against brokers whose data could reveal visits to medical and reproductive health clinics, places of worship and domestic abuse shelters. Its order against X-Mode was the first-ever ban on selling sensitive location data. [12] The bans have gaps. The order against Gravy Analytics and its subsidiary Venntel makes exceptions for national security and law enforcement. [13]

Private buyers use the data too. Senator Ron Wyden found that the Veritas Society, an anti-abortion group, had targeted people who visited 600 Planned Parenthood clinics in 48 states. It used location data from a broker called Near. In Wisconsin alone, it sent 14.3 million ads. Near's chief privacy officer told Wyden's staff that the company had no technical controls against that kind of targeting until the summer of 2022. [14]
"Anonymous" location data isn't
Brokers call the data anonymous because it's tied to an advertising ID instead of a name. Research says otherwise.

A 2013 study looked at 15 months of phone data on 1.5 million people. Four points in time and place were enough to single out 95 percent of them. [15] A 2021 follow-up estimated that in a dataset of 60 million people, four points would still single out 93 percent. Even the authors' most cautious figure, a lower bound, was 22 percent with four points and 87 percent with five. [16] Four points could be your home at night, your office in the morning, your gym after work and a restaurant on Saturday.
Your email is a record, and your accounts are a chain
Email you leave on a provider's servers can sit there for years. In a lawsuit, the other side can demand any of it that's relevant to the case. Police with a warrant can get the messages themselves. [17] [18]

Most people also use one email address, and often one password, for their bank, their doctor's portal and every store they've bought from. When one of those sites is breached, attackers try the same email and password everywhere else. It's called credential stuffing. An old password is the most likely way someone got into the FBI director's personal Gmail, which I go through in my brief on that breach. Have I Been Pwned, a free breach-lookup service, now counts almost 18 billion breached email addresses. [19]

Part III: Why the Law Allows It

Most of this is legal. American privacy law is a patchwork of court rulings and narrow statutes built for phone lines and filing cabinets. Much of it still rests on ideas from the 1970s.

Share it, and you give it up
In 1976, in United States v. Miller, the Supreme Court held that the Fourth Amendment doesn't protect the records your bank keeps about you, because you gave the information to the bank. In 1979, in Smith v. Maryland, it said the same about the phone numbers you dial. [20] [21] That's the third-party doctrine. If you share something with a company, the government can get it from the company without a warrant.

That made more sense for a bank slip or a phone log. Today you can't hold a job, see a doctor or carry a phone without leaving records with someone else. So the same rule now reaches most of the record of a modern life.
The Court has started to pull back
In 2018, in Carpenter v. United States, the Court refused to apply that rule to the location records phone companies keep, because tracking a phone's location gives the government "near perfect surveillance." [22] In June 2026, in Chatrie v. United States, it extended that protection to the location history Google kept for its users. It was "hard to see how any user is, in the normal sense, 'sharing'" that data, the Court said. [23] Both rulings were narrow, and the older cases still stand. So most of what companies hold about you is still treated as shared. I go through these cases in a separate piece.
Your email runs on a 1986 law
The Electronic Communications Privacy Act was written in 1986, before the web existed. Its text still lets the government get email more than 180 days old with a subpoena instead of a warrant. [18] In 2010, a federal appeals court held that the Fourth Amendment protects email content however old it is. [24] In 2016, a bill to update the law passed the House 419 to 0, but it never passed the Senate. [25]

The records around your email get even less protection than the messages themselves. Those records show who you wrote to, when, and from where. That's the metadata problem I come back to in my comparisons of messaging apps and notes apps.
The FTC enforces privacy with a 1938 clause
Congress created the Federal Trade Commission in 1914 to fight monopolies. In 1938 it gave the agency its consumer protection power, a ban on "unfair or deceptive acts or practices." [26] With no federal privacy law, that one clause has become the country's main privacy tool. It reaches more than you might think. The 2024 location cases were brought as unfairness cases, which don't require catching a company in a lie. [12]

But the FTC has no general power to fine a company for a privacy violation, and its orders mostly govern what a company does next. [27] In 2025 the President fired its Democratic commissioners, and in June 2026 the Supreme Court said he could. [28] [29] I go through what the agency can and can't do in my piece on the FTC.
Consent no one can give
Underneath all of this is the idea that you agreed. You clicked "I agree," so what happens next is on you. But in national surveys, most people online believed that a site with a privacy policy wouldn't share their information. That gets it backwards. [30] Reading every privacy policy you encounter would take about 244 hours a year, roughly 40 minutes a day. [31] Solove calls the whole approach privacy self-management. It asks each person to weigh every company's practices one at a time, which no one can do. So the consent it produces "is often not meaningful." [32]
The legal picture A doctrine from the 1970s decides whether your records are protected. A law from 1986 governs your email. An agency founded in 1914 polices privacy with a clause added in 1938. And all of it assumes you read and understood policies no one has time to read. Most of this law wasn't written with the data economy in mind. The parts that try to address it are still catching up.

Part IV: What It Adds Up To

Each piece on its own looks small. The risk comes from putting them together, and courts have started to see it.

The whole reveals more than the parts
For most of its history, Fourth Amendment law judged each piece of information on its own. In 1983, in United States v. Knotts, the Supreme Court held that a person driving on public roads has no reasonable expectation of privacy in where they go. [33] In the 2012 GPS tracking case United States v. Jones, Justice Sotomayor pushed back. Weeks of tracking, she wrote, can reflect "a wealth of detail about her familial, political, professional, religious, and sexual associations." She added, "Awareness that the Government may be watching chills associational and expressive freedoms." [34]

That's the answer to the nothing-to-hide argument in two sentences. One location point is trivial. A month of them shows where you sleep, who you see and which doctor you visit. And knowing you're watched changes what you do. Six years later, in Carpenter, the Court quoted the first of those lines. [22]
What one person's file can hold
Put the sources in this piece together and a stranger can learn a lot about you. A broker gives them your name, address, phone number and relatives. Location data gives them your daily routine. An old email account gives them years of conversations, and past breaches give them working passwords. None of it takes special skills or much money.

An address plus someone with a reason to find you, like an abusive ex or a person with a grudge, becomes a safety problem. That's what happened to Judge Salas's family. A location history plus a detective becomes evidence, even against someone who did nothing, as Jorge Molina learned. An email archive plus a lawsuit becomes material you can't take back.
When it goes wrong, there's little you can do
There's no federal privacy law and no general right to sue over how your data is used. About 20 states have consumer privacy laws in force, and four more take effect by 2028. Only California's lets you sue, and only after certain data breaches. In the rest, only the state can bring a case. [35]

In most places you can't sue a people-search site for listing your address. New Jersey's Daniel's Law, named for Judge Salas's son, is a rare exception. It covers only judges, prosecutors, police officers and a few others. [36] The FTC's location cases ended in orders about what the companies do next, not payments to the people whose data was sold. [12]

So for most people, the protection that works is prevention. That means keeping the data from being collected, or from being easy to find.

Part V: What You Can Do

You can't opt out of the data economy, but you can shrink your part of it. None of these steps is dramatic. Together they close off a lot of what this piece describes.

Limit what your phone shares
Turn off location for apps that don't need it, and precise location for apps that only need your city. Cut off your advertising ID, the tag that lets brokers connect your location from one app to the next. And delete the free apps you don't use. I go through the settings for iPhone and Android in my piece on the government buying data.
Take your address off people-search sites
If you live in California, one request through DROP tells every registered broker to delete you and keep deleting you. Everywhere else, you opt out one site at a time. Either way, listings built from public records stay up until you remove them yourself. They also come back, so search for yourself again every few months. I go through what DROP removes and what it leaves behind in my piece on DROP.
Break the chain between your accounts
Give every account its own long password. A password manager makes that practical. Add a passkey or a security key wherever you can. Check every email address you use at Have I Been Pwned, and replace any password you used with an address that shows up there. [19] The FBI director's case shows what happens when this gets skipped.
Keep less, and keep it encrypted
Delete old email you don't need, since what isn't stored can't be demanded, bought or leaked. When you choose tools, pick ones that collect less and encrypt more. In other pieces, I compare email providers, messaging apps, notes apps and photo services, and explain how encryption works.
Look yourself up first
Search your name, your address and your phone number the way a stranger would, and see what comes up. It's the quickest way to learn which of these steps matter most for you.

Conclusion

None of the people in this piece was hiding anything. What hurt them was information about them in someone else's hands.

Who decides what your data means
Privacy protects ordinary people from ordinary information used against them. Right now, for most Americans, companies collect by default and brokers sell what they gather. The government can buy what it would need a warrant to take. The law is starting to move, slowly, with Chatrie, California's DROP and new state laws.

Until the law catches up, the information that can't be used against you is the information that was never collected, or that you've already cleaned up. Most people have never seen the whole picture of what's out there about them. That picture is where any real fix begins.
