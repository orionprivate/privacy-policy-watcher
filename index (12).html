The FTC was never enough: How America's de facto privacy regulator was structurally outmatched long before it lost its independence

For two decades the Federal Trade Commission was the closest thing Americans had to a privacy regulator. In 2025 the President fired its Democratic commissioners, and in June 2026 the Supreme Court held that he could. The easy story is that politics broke a working agency. The truer one is that the FTC that Congress built could not have protected your privacy on its own, even at its best. Its most careful scholar, Chris Hoofnagle, documented why while defending it.

The United States has no federal privacy law. Where the European Union has the GDPR and independent regulators, the United States has a patchwork. HIPAA covers health, FERPA covers education, COPPA covers children, and almost everything else falls to the Federal Trade Commission.

Its privacy authority comes from Section 5 of the FTC Act, which bars "unfair or deceptive acts or practices." That's the whole of it. There's no privacy mandate, no general power to fine a privacy violation, and no practical way to write general privacy rules. The role was inferred from a commercial fairness statute a century older than the data economy.

The FTC made it work by improvising. Its staff framed privacy cases as deception and settled them in consent orders that bound Google, Facebook and others for twenty years. A consent order is a settlement the agency issues itself. When a company breaks one, the penalty case goes to federal court, where the settlement is usually called a consent decree.

The approach produced real results, and it was fragile and insufficient at the same time. It was fragile because it depended on lawyers willing to push, economists willing to go along, and commissioners willing to vote yes. It was insufficient because the tools were never built for the job. The second problem matters more, and it's the one I argue here.

Two more threads run through what follows. Section 5 reaches commerce, not the government. Yet the government has become one of the largest buyers of the data you generate. And the agency's history keeps teaching one distinction. A promise protects you until someone decides otherwise. A constraint protects you until someone acts, in public, to remove it. The FTC spent most of its privacy history policing promises, and in the end it was only a promise itself.

Part I: Borrowed Authority

The FTC was built in 1914 to break up monopolies. Privacy came more than eighty years later. The agency had to improvise it out of a fairness statute and a long history of false advertising cases. Its most careful scholar, Chris Hoofnagle, is my main source, and that's deliberate. He wrote the strongest defense of the agency, and the same body of work holds the clearest evidence of its limits. I read them together.

Built to fight monopolies
Congress created the FTC in 1914 to fight the trusts and monopolies of the industrial economy. It had no consumer protection mission until the Wheeler-Lea Amendments of 1938 added "unfair or deceptive acts or practices" to its original ban on "unfair methods of competition." [1] Privacy is a small, recent entry on a long list. The agency enforces some seventy laws, covering everything from "dolphin-safe" tuna labels to the sanctioning of boxing matches.

Hoofnagle describes the FTC as an exercise in "creative syncretism," an agency given many flexible tools so it could adapt to problems Congress couldn't foresee. [1] That flexibility is its strength. For privacy it's also a weakness, because a regulator designed to be redirected can be redirected by whoever holds it.

There's an irony here. The core of the modern privacy problem is bigness, the thing the FTC was created to fight. Louis Brandeis called concentrated economic power the "curse of bigness." Hoofnagle traces a line from that fight to today's calls for the FTC to police the power that comes from piling up personal data. [1] The agency's original job held a natural home for privacy.
Borrowed from advertising law
Section 5 gives the agency two tools. Under the FTC's 1983 Policy Statement on Deception, a practice is deceptive when a representation, omission or practice is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably and the point is material. The test for unfairness comes from a 1980 Policy Statement, which Congress wrote into the statute in 1994. A practice is unfair when it causes substantial injury that consumers can't reasonably avoid and that isn't outweighed by benefits to consumers or competition. [2] The gap between those two tests shaped the agency's entire privacy practice.

Hoofnagle's central legal claim is that this authority is broader than most people realize. He argues that the FTC could go further on privacy by drawing on its long record in advertising cases. [1] Congress chose vague language in 1914 and again in 1938 so the statute could grow.

But the flexibility that let staff reach new privacy practices also meant the agency's privacy law grew out of false advertising cases. Those cases don't map cleanly onto collecting and selling personal data. The authority was real but secondhand, and it had a fixed edge. Section 5 reaches commerce, which means companies and not the government.

Authority borrowed this way lasts only as long as the agency keeps choosing to use it, which makes it closer to a promise than a constraint.

Part II: Even at Full Strength

For this part, set 2025 aside and picture the FTC at its best, fully funded and bipartisan, with committed lawyers and commissioners who want aggressive privacy enforcement. Two of the three limits below are choices Congress made, not failures of the agency, so the claim needs to be sized carefully. I'm not saying no Congress could build an adequate privacy agency. I'm saying the one Congress did build couldn't be made adequate by better leadership, more money or more will.

What adequate would mean
A test that can't fail isn't worth much, so here is what adequate would mean. An agency equal to the data economy would need four things.

1. The power to set rules in advance, on a timetable that works.
2. A penalty for the first violation, not only the second.
3. The reach to stop a harmful practice whether or not the company promised anything about it.
4. Protection that outlasts a change in leadership, meaning precedent a court has ratified and orders a later commission can't quietly drop.

This part tests the first three, and Parts IV and V test the fourth. If the FTC had these powers, or could have used them as built, my argument fails. The agency did achieve a lot. It brought more than 150 privacy and security cases and shaped how an entire industry behaved. [3] The question is whether even its best met this bar.

I've left one gap out of the test on purpose. Section 5 can't reach the government, so grading the FTC on that would grade it on something it was never built to do. Part VI comes back to it.
No rules in advance
A regulator that can't set rules in advance can only punish conduct after it happens, one company at a time. For most privacy questions, that's the FTC's position.

To issue a binding rule under Section 5, it has to follow Section 18 of the FTC Act. Congress created that process in the Magnuson-Moss Act of 1975. It made the process heavier in 1980, after the agency's attempt to restrict children's television advertising set off a backlash. [4] The process adds an advance notice, informal hearings and a formal economic analysis to ordinary notice and comment. Rules made this way have taken almost six years on average, against under a year for ordinary rules. [5]

When Hoofnagle wrote in 2016, the agency "has not sought to promulgate rules for privacy." [1] It finally tried in August 2022, under Chair Lina Khan, with a rulemaking on commercial surveillance and data security that never got past the advance notice. [6] And the process can undo the rules the agency does finish. In July 2025 a federal appeals court threw out the FTC's click-to-cancel rule because the agency had skipped a required preliminary economic analysis. [7]

The exceptions matter. Where Congress handed the agency a specific privacy rule, it has one. The COPPA Rule protects children under 13, the Safeguards Rule covers data security at financial companies under the FTC's jurisdiction, and the Health Breach Notification Rule covers health apps. [8] But no general FTC rule tells companies what they may do with your data before they do it. Everywhere else there is only enforcement after the fact.
No penalty for a first violation
Under Section 5, the first time a company misuses your data, the FTC can't fine it. It can only order the company to stop. Money comes into it only if the company later breaks that order, breaks a trade regulation rule, or breaks a statute with its own penalties, such as COPPA. [2]

That's why the famous privacy penalties were both for breaking consent orders the companies had already agreed to. Google paid $22.5 million in 2012, and Facebook paid $5 billion in 2019. In effect the first violation is free. When a penalty comes only on the second offense, and is small next to a large company's revenue, that company has little reason to be careful the first time.

Section 5 does offer one route to a first-violation fine, and the agency's habit of settling closed it. Under Section 5(m)(1)(B), the FTC can fine a company that knowingly engages in a practice the Commission has already found unlawful in an earlier case. But the earlier order has to come out of a contested case, because the statute excludes consent orders by name. [2] Since nearly every privacy case ended in a consent order, the privacy docket produced almost nothing this route could use.

Where Congress wrote a specific rule, the picture changes. The FTC can fine a first violation of the COPPA Rule. In 2023 GoodRx agreed to a $1.5 million penalty for sharing users' prescription and health information with advertisers. It was the first penalty under the Health Breach Notification Rule. [9] A 2024 law lets the FTC seek penalties against data brokers that sell Americans' sensitive data to China, Russia, Iran, North Korea or companies they control. [10] Congress has raised the ceiling for children, health records and national security, but not for privacy in general.

The one general workaround was then taken away. For decades the FTC used Section 13(b) of the Act to win restitution directly in federal court. In AMG Capital Management v. FTC (2021), a unanimous Supreme Court held that Section 13(b) authorizes no such relief, because Congress had never granted it. [11]
Deterrence without fines
Money isn't the only thing that deters a company. A twenty-year order brings audits and monitoring, and an FTC investigation can move a stock price. Solove and Hartzog argue that the settlement system disciplined companies in exactly these ways. [12] That's real, but every one of those pressures depends on the agency's continuing willingness to investigate, sue and monitor. That willingness can change the moment the agency changes hands. One deterrent depends on no one's continuing will, a price fixed in advance for the first offense. That's exactly what the statute withholds.
Mostly against broken promises
Of Section 5's two tests, unfairness requires proof of substantial injury and a weighing of costs against benefits. Deception requires only a misleading statement that matters. The agency's economists treated privacy injury as unmeasurable without a market price. So privacy enforcement came to rest mostly on deception, which means catching companies breaking their own promises. [3]

A company that never promised you anything had nothing to break. Platforms learned to work that gap. They paid app developers in user data while truthfully enough denying that they "sold" it. Hoofnagle showed that this was a sale by barter, which the deception model couldn't easily touch. [13] The incentive taught companies to manage what they said rather than what they collected.

Experts the agency itself invited had already shown that the promise model didn't work. Joseph Turow, Hoofnagle, Deirdre Mulligan and their colleagues wrote a 2006 paper for the FTC's Tech-ade hearings. It showed that the FTC had adopted only part of the Fair Information Practices. The agency kept notice, choice, access and security, and dropped the parts that carried the weight. Those were limits on collection and use, purpose specification and accountability. The omission, they wrote, "led firms to collect extraneous information, and repurpose information without consumer consent." About 59 percent of people surveyed wrongly believed that a site with a "privacy policy" would not share their data. Only 1.4 percent said they read software license agreements thoroughly. [14]

Notice and choice also changed what privacy meant. Justice Harlan's test in Katz v. United States asks whether a person actually expected privacy and whether society is prepared to recognize that expectation as reasonable. [15] The second question sets the limit from outside any transaction. Notice and choice moved the question inside it. If the policy disclosed the practice and you clicked accept, the practice was legitimate.

The Fourth Amendment drifted the same way in the third-party cases, which held that what you knowingly share with a phone company or a bank loses protection. [15] The agency built its enforcement on a theory of privacy that was already shifting from constraint to permission.

Still, the promise limit was a habit more than a wall. From 2022 to 2024 the agency brought unfairness cases against companies that collected and sold sensitive location data. They included X-Mode, Gravy Analytics, Mobilewalla and Kochava. The cases were built on the harm itself rather than on any broken promise. [16] The agency could reach past promises when its leadership chose to. The same discretion could just as easily stop.
Norms, the agency's real tool
The FTC never governed mainly by fines, and Hoofnagle insists on the point. Its power was in setting norms and forcing information into the open. Its Section 6(b) study power let it compel companies to hand over what they'd rather keep private. That power produced a 2024 staff report on nine large social media and streaming platforms. [17] Through workshops, reports and guidance on how to handle your data, it set the terms companies believed they had to meet. This is the "creative syncretism" Hoofnagle admires, and he's right about it. [1]

But a norm is the most fragile tool an agency has. A consent order at least binds a company for twenty years and takes a formal proceeding to undo. Guidance binds no one, and it disappears when the agency stops saying it. In March 2025 the FTC took down more than three hundred blog posts from the prior administration, with no public explanation. They included business guidance on AI, consumer protection and privacy. [18]

Rules, first-violation penalties and the reach to stop harmful practices would have been constraints. What the FTC had were promises to bring the next case, keep the guidance up and hold the line. None of them bound the next commission.

Part III: The Inner Struggle

Even the narrow, deception-based enforcement the FTC could manage had to get past its own economists. In "The Federal Trade Commission's Inner Privacy Struggle," Hoofnagle documented a dynamic most privacy advocates never saw. In separate work he supplied the economics that explains why it mattered.

Economists who priced privacy at zero
Inside the FTC, lawyers in the Bureau of Consumer Protection choose and build cases. Economists from the Bureau of Economics evaluate them and recommend remedies. Hoofnagle showed that the economists consistently concluded that privacy violations cause no measurable harm, which pushed recommended penalties toward zero. [3]

Without a market price for privacy, the Bureau sees no basis for measuring injury. Google was fined $22.5 million for tracking Safari users it had promised not to track. An economist could see even that as too high, because no consumer had paid for the privacy feature Google got around. In that view, "any price on the tracking would be speculative."

Hoofnagle obtained the Bureau's training materials through a Freedom of Information Act request. He found a reading list with "a distinctly laissez faire bent," led by a paper from an industry-funded think tank. [3] The economists were not corrupt. They were trained to find what they found.

The economists' view has serious defenders. James Cooper and Joshua Wright both held senior posts at the FTC. In the same Cambridge handbook that holds Hoofnagle's chapter, they argued that the agency's privacy work lacked the economic discipline that reshaped antitrust in the 1970s. They wanted privacy harms tied to consumer welfare and enforcement backed by evidence. [19] That's a fair demand, since an agency that sues without a clear theory of harm invites courts to strike it down. Where I part ways is over what counts as harm.

The statute doesn't require that privacy injury be reduced to a dollar figure. Section 5 asks about substantial injury, not out-of-pocket loss. Hoofnagle argues that the cognizable-harm requirement that critics press "does not exist anywhere in Title 15." [1] Losing control over information about you is an injury on its own terms.

In 2024 a federal court agreed, at least at the start of a case. In the FTC's suit against the location data broker Kochava, it held that "an invasion of privacy may constitute an injury that gives rise to liability under Section 5(a)." [20] Insisting that privacy harm doesn't count until it can be priced is a policy choice dressed up as arithmetic.
The economics the Bureau got backward
Hoofnagle and the economist Jan Whittington offered a stronger rebuttal. Applying transaction cost economics to "free" services, they found that a zero-price exchange is still a market transaction, and an unusually costly one. [21] As you pay a service with your data, you become dependent on it and can't leave without losing what you've built there. The exchange is continuous, like a long-term contract with a company that keeps changing its terms. Its costs are mostly hidden from you when you sign up. The market the Bureau says doesn't exist plainly does, and the Bureau has declined to price it.

The agency's own 2024 study drew on answers compelled from nine of the largest platforms. It documented an industry built on collecting personal information and monetizing it at scale. [17] One part of the agency measured what another had called unmeasurable. No single person's data has a clean price, and I'm not claiming one. The narrower point is enough. Personal information was producing measurable revenue. An economics that looked only for the consumer's out-of-pocket loss was looking in the wrong place.

The lock-in is also the moat. A zero price lets a platform grow fast and wall out competitors. That's why Hoofnagle and Whittington argue that "free" has to become part of how competition law understands platform power. [21]
The single point of failure
The agency's lawyers adapted. Unfairness invites the cost-benefit analysis the economists used to zero out harm, and deception doesn't. So they pleaded privacy cases as deception wherever a broken promise allowed it. "Deception cases receive much less economic attention," Hoofnagle noted. [3]

This worked because the FTC gives staff lawyers wide freedom to choose cases. Its investigative power is, in Hoofnagle's phrase, "akin to an inquisitorial body." [1] That insulation came from staff discretion plus commissioners protected from political pressure. He saw it as the agency's defense against shifting politics. It was also its single point of failure. Privacy enforcement happened as long as the lawyers controlled which cases got brought.

Hoofnagle identified the load-bearing wall. He didn't say, and may not have expected, that someone could simply remove it. That control was a promise the structure made to its staff, and the structure could take it back.

Part IV: Settlements, Not Law

The consent order made sense given how little else the FTC had. But it was a poor foundation for anything lasting, and the courts started exposing its weaknesses well before any administration did.

Law no court had ratified
The consent orders did produce law. Solove and Hartzog showed, and Hoofnagle agrees, that they added up to a "new common law of privacy" that companies treated as binding. [12] Companies hired lawyers, built compliance programs and behaved as if the settlements were precedent. But after more than fifteen years of enforcement there was, in their words, "no meaningful body of judicial decisions to show for it," because nearly every case settled. [12] As Hoofnagle put it from the inside, the wrongness of a practice "is simply declared in a complaint and consent decree agreed to by the respondent." [3]

A common law no court has entered binds only by convention, and conventions can be dropped. Van Eijk, Hoofnagle and Kannekens note that "the contours of the FTC Act are unclear," because the agency rarely explains why a practice is unfair or deceptive. [22] That vagueness helped while the agency was expanding. Once leadership chose to pull back, there was no judicial precedent to overrule and nothing to stop the retreat.
What the courts did say
In the years after Solove and Hartzog wrote, the courts said two things, and they pointed in different directions. In FTC v. Wyndham Worldwide (2015), the Third Circuit upheld the agency's power to treat weak data security as unfair. In LabMD v. FTC (2018), the Eleventh Circuit threw out an FTC data security order because its command to keep a "reasonable" security program was too vague to enforce. [23] The courts confirmed the power and then limited what the agency could order with it.

LabMD traces back to Part II. With no data security rule written in advance, the agency had only the open language of unfairness to work with. Its order was open-ended too. The inability to make rules weakened the enforcement that was supposed to substitute for them. Then AMG took away the agency's main route to money. [11] The courts were wearing down the consent order model before any president touched it. And an order the agency issues is one the agency can reopen.
Too late to matter
Hoofnagle also saw a timing problem. "Remedies are unlikely to be effective by the time the FTC gets involved, investigates a case, and litigates it," he wrote. The delay "gives respondents time to establish their platform and shut out competitors." [3] Facebook and Google both won you over with privacy promises, then relaxed them once you couldn't easily leave. By the time an order issued, it governed yesterday's practice. An enforcer that can act only years after the fact is not a constraint on a fast-moving industry. It is a historian of it.
The competition route The agency's founding power, tested in court.
The agency still had its older tool. The lock-in described in Part III is also a competition harm, and competition is the FTC's founding mission. But American antitrust asks, at bottom, whether a company can raise prices above the competitive level. A zero-price product scrambles that question at the root. [21]

The FTC pressed the theory anyway. It sued to unwind Meta's purchases of Instagram and WhatsApp. In November 2025 the court ruled for Meta. The agency had proposed a narrow market of personal social networking. It covered Facebook, Instagram, Snapchat and the small network MeWe. The court held that the market had to include TikTok and YouTube, which pushed Meta's share below monopoly levels. [24] With no price to measure, the court measured time and attention. A market drawn that way can stretch to include anything else competing for your attention.

The FTC appealed in January 2026. Among other things, it argued that the court gave too much weight to recent changes in the market. It also argued that TikTok doesn't serve the friends-and-family sharing at the center of the case. The appeal is pending in the D.C. Circuit. [24]

One district court loss isn't a structural verdict. The market may have been framed badly, the appeal may succeed, and the industry did change over the five years the case ran. But the ruling shows the doctrine at work. Even on the ground the agency was built for, its tools were made to see prices, and the platform economy doesn't charge you any.

Part V: The Test, 2025 to 2026

Hoofnagle's 2017 essay ended with a prediction. He expected the Bureau of Economics to gain influence under a Trump administration. That would push cases toward unfairness and blunt the lawyers' expansion of privacy norms. He was right about the direction. But his own prescription was to strengthen the agency and defend it against an "ideological movement intent on disassembling the administrative state." He warned that the movement was "more radical than their sponsors understand." [1] What happened in 2025 and 2026 went well past the internal rebalancing he predicted.

What Hoofnagle predicted
His forecast was internal and procedural. The economists would gain influence over which cases got brought, and unfairness would become the favored theory. Privacy cases would get harder to bring, because the economists would demand proof of harm their methods were built not to find. Enforcement would shrink because the balance of power inside the agency changed, not because the law did. The prediction assumed the institution would stay intact and the contest would still be fought inside it, under its usual procedures.
What happened in 2025 This went beyond what Hoofnagle predicted.
In January 2025 Andrew Ferguson became chairman. He's no relation to the legal scholar Andrew Guthrie Ferguson. Punchbowl News reported on a document it described as his pitch for the job. It promised to drop "novel and legally dubious consumer protection cases." [25] Weeks earlier, in the location data cases against Gravy Analytics and Mobilewalla, he had written, "My colleagues want the FTC Act to be a comprehensive privacy law. But it is not." [26] He shares my premise and draws the opposite conclusion. In February he named Christopher Mufarrige to lead the Bureau of Consumer Protection. [25]

In March, President Trump fired the two Democratic commissioners, Alvaro Bedoya and Rebecca Kelly Slaughter. Both sued, and after Bedoya resigned in June, Slaughter carried the case to the Supreme Court. [27] That same March the agency took down more than three hundred blog posts from the prior administration. [18]

In December 2025 the FTC reopened and set aside its own consent order against Rytr, a company that sold an AI writing tool. The order had been issued under former Chair Lina Khan over Ferguson's dissent. The Commission started the reopening itself. It cited the administration's AI Action Plan. The plan directed the agency to review "all FTC final orders, consent decrees, and injunctions" and set aside any "that unduly burden AI innovation." [28] The case was about AI, not privacy, but the mechanism matters. A final consent order can now be undone by a commission acting on a White House policy.
What happened in 2026
On June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court decided Trump v. Slaughter, 6 to 3. It held that the FTC Act's protection against removing commissioners violates the separation of powers. As applied to the FTC, it overruled Humphrey's Executor, the 1935 decision that had upheld that protection. The President can now fire commissioners at will. The dissent warned that the law's bipartisan requirement "can easily be evaded simply by firing all Commissioners of the opposite party." [29]

That protection was the one real constraint shielding the FTC from political control. The Court had upheld it in 1935. Removing it took a public fight that ran fifteen months through three courts. Everything built on top of it came down faster. The guidance came down the same month as the firings, with no explanation. The Rytr order went at the agency's own initiative. That's the difference between a constraint and a promise, playing out on the agency itself.

Commissioner Melissa Holyoak left in November 2025 to become interim U.S. Attorney for Utah. Since then the commission has had two members, Ferguson and Mark Meador, with a third nominee waiting on the Senate. In mid-2026 they split 1-1 over a merger review, and the tie ended the review with no action. [30] Either commissioner can now stop any case.

In May 2026 X Corp. asked the FTC to end Twitter's 2022 privacy order early. The order came with a $150 million penalty. If you gave Twitter your phone number to secure your account, that order is what bars the company from using that number to target ads. It runs until 2042, and X wants it to end in 2026. Comments closed July 2, and the FTC had not decided as of this update. [31] This is a privacy order, the exact tool Part IV describes. Whether it survives depends on two commissioners the President can remove at will.

The case against the location data broker Kochava, filed in 2022, led to a proposed settlement in May 2026. It bars Kochava from selling sensitive location data without consent, and it includes no payment that I could find. According to one analysis, it's narrower than the 2024 X-Mode order. [32] The theory survived the change in leadership in a smaller form.

The current FTC hasn't stopped protecting privacy. It calls children's privacy a top priority, and its updated children's privacy rule became enforceable in April 2026. In 2025 Disney agreed to pay $10 million to settle charges that it mislabeled children's videos on YouTube. [33] In February 2026 it warned data brokers against selling Americans' data to foreign adversaries. [10] In August 2025 Ferguson warned tech companies that weakening encryption at the request of foreign governments may violate Section 5. [34] What changed is which parts of your privacy get protected, and whoever holds the agency now decides that.
Beyond the prediction
Hoofnagle predicted a shift inside the agency's structure. What happened was the removal of the structure's protections. He saw the FTC's shield as staff control of case selection and commissioners the President couldn't fire at will. The events of 2025 overrode both, and Slaughter erased the second as a matter of law. The lawyers brought more than 150 privacy and security cases and won orders against Google, Facebook, Twitter and Microsoft. That shows what the people inside could do. [3] How quickly it all stopped shows what the structure couldn't protect.

Part VI: What Enough Looks Like

It's tempting to read all of this as one long staircase down. Hoofnagle's own history corrects that. The FTC's power has always moved in cycles. In the late 1970s the press called it a "National Nanny" after it tried to restrict children's advertising. In 1980 Congress let its funding lapse in the middle of a fight over its powers. The agency closed for a day, and Congress then limited its rulemaking. [4] [35] It recovered to become what Hoofnagle calls the most important regulator of information privacy in the world. [1] This low point may pass too.

What the cycle doesn't change
What doesn't move with the cycle is the ceiling. At the top of the cycle and at the bottom, the agency couldn't fine a first violation or write a general privacy rule. It built little precedent a court would enforce, and it depended on discretion that could be redirected. The politics will swing back. The ceiling moves only by statute, and so far Congress has raised it only for children, health records and national security.
Hoofnagle's answer, supervision
Hoofnagle saw the ceiling himself, and his answer matters because it isn't the one I end on. He concedes that modern information problems may not be policeable by an enforcement agency at all. He points instead to supervision, the continuous oversight that bank examiners and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau perform. Supervision comes with standing access, regular audits and the power to correct practices before they cause harm. [1] It doesn't wait for a broken promise or need a first-violation penalty, and it sets expectations in advance. If the institutional path has a future, this is probably it, and it should be built.

But supervision still depends on who's in charge. Examiners report to a chair, and Congress can take back what it granted. Some institutions do outlast the people running them. Courts are bound by precedent. Central banks are anchored by statute and markets, and even Slaughter set the Federal Reserve apart. Civil servants have tenure protections. [29] The FTC's privacy work had none of those anchors. It was discretion all the way down. A privacy regime built on someone's continuing will lasts only as long as that will does. Institutions matter. The question is which kinds of institutional power survive a change of hands.
Testing the benchmark
Apply the benchmark from Part II to real alternatives and it turns out it can be met, at least in part.

California comes closest. Its privacy agency, CalPrivacy, writes binding regulations and fines first violations. The latest regulations took effect in January 2026. Honda paid $632,500, Tractor Supply $1.35 million and General Motors $12.75 million in a joint action with the attorney general. Data brokers have been fined just for failing to register. [36] Your rights there apply whatever a company promised. One request through DROP, the state's deletion platform, now reaches every registered data broker. I go through what DROP does and doesn't remove in a separate piece.

The law's durability has an anchor too. Because voters passed it as Proposition 24, the legislature can amend it only in ways that are "consistent with and further the purpose and intent" of the act. [37] What it lacks is much court-tested precedent, and like any state law it's exposed to federal preemption.

Texas has shown what first-violation money looks like under state law. In 2025 Google agreed to a $1.375 billion settlement over location tracking, private browsing and biometric data. [38]

The federal bill in Congress cuts both ways. The SECURE Data Act was introduced in the House in April 2026. It would give you rights to access, correct and delete your data and to opt out of targeted advertising and data sales. It would also treat violations as violations of an FTC rule, which would allow penalties for a first violation.

But it also gives companies a mandatory 45-day period to cure a violation before anyone can enforce, which gives back much of that. And it preempts state law so broadly that Representative Frank Pallone said at the June hearing it would reach California's broker deletion law. [39] If you live in California, it could leave you with less than you have now.
The half the agency never faced
Every option above shares one more gap. None of them reaches the government, which has become one of the largest buyers of the data your devices produce. The state doesn't need to break into your phone when it can buy your location history from a broker. Carpenter v. United States requires a warrant for location records compelled from a phone carrier and says nothing about records bought on the open market.

Some federal laws limit government access, such as the Stored Communications Act and the Privacy Act. But none of them stops an agency from buying from a broker. Two bills would close that gap, the Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act and the Government Surveillance Reform Act. Neither has passed. [40] I cover this in my piece on the government buying data it cannot legally collect. On the commercial side the FTC was never enough. On the government side it was never present.

Part VII: Promises and Constraints

If every institutional answer depends on who holds office, the remaining question is whether anything protects you that doesn't. There's one candidate, and it's the distinction I started with.

A constraint built into the product
A privacy policy is a promise the company can revoke. In a zero-knowledge design, the company never holds the keys to your data. That's a constraint it can't simply revoke. A provider that holds no keys can't be compelled to hand over what it can't read. It can't be breached into data it never had, and it can't sell what it never collected. It also can't quietly loosen a setting to expose you. Facebook did exactly that in December 2009, changing users' privacy settings without their approval, and the change led to its first FTC order. [41]

Against a sale and against a subpoena, the protection that lasts no matter who's in office is the one written into the design. The mechanics are in my piece on how encryption actually works.
Who carries the burden
There's an obvious objection. Part II argued that notice and choice failed because individuals can't protect themselves. It would be inconsistent to then tell you to go install the right apps. That puts the burden back on the individual and sorts protection by sophistication and income, giving the most privacy to the people who need help least. Turow and Hoofnagle's survey work found that public passivity about privacy is a knowledge problem, not apathy. [14] People care. The system is built so that caring isn't enough.

So the argument has to be made one level up. The goal is for the constraint to be how products are built, rather than an upgrade that sophisticated users go looking for. It should be the default that ships in the box. It should also be the floor that a procurement contract or a future privacy law requires, and the standard a regulator would set if it could still set rules.
What airbags teach
Airbags are the closest comparison I know, and they cut against the easy version of this argument. An airbag protects you whether or not you understand it, and no settings update can switch it off. But airbags didn't become standard because drivers demanded them or carmakers volunteered them. Federal regulators pushed for passive restraints through years of industry resistance. Congress finally required airbags in every new passenger car built after August 1997. [42]

Web encryption spread through standards bodies. After 2013 it also spread because large platforms found it useful. So far, constraint at scale has arrived through mandates or corporate self-interest, the same forces I've spent this whole piece distrusting. Architecture doesn't escape politics when it's adopted. What it changes is how protection lasts once it's built, and what it costs to remove.
The limits of the answer
I've used Hoofnagle as someone who defended the FTC while documenting what held it back. I'm doing the same with encryption, so its limits come first, and the claim I make is whatever survives them.

The first limit is that architecture is itself regulated. The Clipper chip of the 1990s was a key-escrow mandate built into hardware. Its logic returns in lawful-access proposals and in client-side scanning, which would inspect content on your device before encryption applies. In January 2025 the United Kingdom served Apple a secret order demanding access to end-to-end encrypted iCloud data. Apple withdrew its Advanced Data Protection feature from British users rather than comply. In 2026 Britain served a new notice limited to UK users, and Apple challenged it in August. In September a tribunal heard whether the government can keep such orders secret at all. [43]

That secrecy fight is the real test of my claim. Apple held no key it could hand over quietly, so the state had to order it to change what it builds. The order surfaced in press reports. Apple answered in public by pulling the feature, which turned it into a dispute between two governments. The UK's effort to keep such orders secret shows how much the state prefers to act quietly.

A promise can be broken in a settings update no one reads. A constraint can be overridden only by forcing a visible change, a feature pulled or a product rebuilt, where it can be seen and contested.

The second limit is scope. End-to-end encryption protects what you say, not necessarily the metadata around it. Most services keep a great deal of metadata. Encryption does nothing once the device itself is seized or hacked. And it runs into compulsion. Courts are divided on whether the Fifth Amendment lets the state force you to decrypt a device. In 2016 a federal court used the All Writs Act to order Apple to help unlock an iPhone. And border agents can search devices on little or no suspicion. [44]

So here is the claim, stated precisely. Architecture doesn't put protection beyond politics, and nothing does. What it changes is the default and the cost of reversal. A guidance page dies by deletion, a consent order by a commission vote, and an enforcement priority by an election. Each of those is quiet, cheap and fast. A deployed constraint dies only by an act that leaves a visible mark. That could be a law passed, an order that forces a product change, or an update pushed to every device. It changes what the state must do to reach you, and how visibly it has to do it.

Conclusion

The FTC was never enough because of how Congress built it, not because of who ran it. Even at its peak it couldn't write a general privacy rule or fine a first violation. It reached past promises only when its leaders chose to, and a later commission could drop its protections. It was never built to reach the government at all. In 2025 and 2026 those limits stopped being theoretical. Commissioners can now be fired at will, and the agency is down to two members. A privacy order meant to last until 2042 is up for early termination.

What lasts
None of this argues against institutions. Supervision is worth building, and California shows the benchmark can be met in part. A federal law could raise the ceiling too, if it doesn't trade away stronger state protections to do it. But every institutional answer shares the FTC's basic weakness. It lasts only as long as the people holding it choose to keep it.

The protection that survives a change of administration is not the one beyond the state's reach, because nothing is. It's the one the state must act in public to remove, provided we insist it be built in for everyone and stay honest about what it covers. The math doesn't change with the administration. But the math was never the whole of it, and saying so is what makes the case worth trusting.
